Three deals most agents would have walked away from.

None of these were the easy version. One client had been turned down by four separate lenders. One wanted a house that was already under contract with somebody else and sitting in foreclosure. One was a single mom with three months of work history in this country.

Here is exactly what happened in each, including the parts that took months and the numbers at the end.

Case one

Four lenders said no. They had keys in three months.

Mary was a researcher at the University of Utah. Her husband was self-employed. Between them they had real income coming in every month, and by the time we met they had been turned down four separate times.

A local broker with a great reputation. A big national lender. Their longtime credit union. And a private investor charging high interest, who still said no.

The problem was never whether they earned enough. Mary was paid through a stipend rather than a conventional salary, and most lenders do not know what to do with that. Her husband's self-employment income created the same difficulty from the other direction. Two legitimate incomes, and four institutions that could not make either of them fit the box on the form.

They had almost given up.

I told one of their family members, "I am not going to rest until I see you guys in your own home."

I meant it. When somebody tells me it will not work, the only question I am interested in is "How can we get it done and who can help?".

We started working together in early February. On May 15th they got their keys.

Here is what actually changed, because it was not motivation and it was not luck.

It was the network. Early in my career I met a loan officer with years of experience who reads past the surface of a file. He looked at their financials differently than the four institutions that had already passed on them. Same income, same documents, a different set of eyes.

Then we found a builder with the right home, in the right location, offering financing you do not see every day.

Most agents would have moved on from this file. There was no strong pre-approval letter at the start, and that is exactly the point where the easy version of a deal disappears. I took it as a personal challenge instead.

Now, instead of paying into somebody else's investment portfolio through their monthly rent, they are building equity in their own.

What I want you to take from this is simple. Do not let a bank, a credit union, or a well-meaning family member decide what is possible for you. There are more options out there than most people know about, and more than some professionals know about.

Keep asking who can help me with this and how can it be done.

I cannot promise you anything without knowing your situation, and I will tell you plainly if the honest answer is not yet. But if we work together, I am not going to accept a no on your behalf until we have actually run out of options.

Case two

Listed at $1,195,000. My clients closed at $925,000.

$975,000Contract price
$916,000Appraisal, $59,000 under contract
$925,000Closed, with $5,000 in concessions

My clients were relocating from out of state for a job. Out of every home they toured, this was the one that checked every single box.

The timing was wrong in every direction. They were months away from being ready to buy, contingent on selling their own home first, and the property was already under contract with somebody else.

Most buyers move on at that point. We did not.

I started digging into the listing history and talking to the listing agent. The home had been listed at $1.3 million a year earlier. It had gone under contract multiple times and every attempt to sell had fallen through, including one buyer who was also contingent on selling their own home.

So I kept digging, and two more things turned up.

The layout was unusual enough that comparable sales could not produce a clean market value. And the sellers had a hard money lien on the property, which meant real financial pressure and a clock running on them.

That changed the position completely.

Before we ever went under contract, we submitted three offers, all close to list price. Every time, the listing agent told us it would not work for the sellers. We never received an official counter.

I told my clients not to react out of frustration. We needed to wait and put together an effective strategy.

We did not go under contract until the hard money lender foreclosed and took the property back.

It went to auction at the courthouse. I was there in person early that morning while my clients were still out of state, working on the sale of their own home. The opening bid was too high, so nobody bid. That is when we stepped in and negotiated directly with the lender.

The lender would not go below $975,000, which was still high according to my own research.

My clients wanted the home. They did not want to overpay. Because I was confident in my valuation, I suggested we offer full price and let the appraisal contingency do the work.

The appraisal came back at $916,000, fifty-nine thousand under contract.

The lender was not willing to drop the price, so I laid out plainly what would happen if the home went back on the market. Close to a year of listing history with no sale, and multiple failed contracts behind it, was not going to produce a better outcome for them.

We closed at $925,000, with $5,000 in closing costs covered for my clients. That is $270,000 below the price it was listed at when we started, and well below the $1.3 million it had been asking a year before that.

Most buyers would have been intimidated somewhere in that process and walked away, even with a good agent beside them. Years of martial arts and coaching high performers taught me the same lesson over and over. Nothing great comes from comfort.

Price is the last thing that gets decided in a deal like this. Everything that comes before it is information and timing.

Case three

Three lenders told her she could not qualify. She closed anyway.

Carin is a widowed single mom. By the time we met, three different lenders had already told her she could not qualify for a mortgage.

She had a clear goal, and she wasn't willing to back out from it. She wanted to start house hacking inside of twelve months, meaning buy a home, rent out part of it, and let that income carry the mortgage. When I saw that kind of motivation I committed to helping her get there.

Her situation was unusual, and plenty of agents would have walked away from it. When she applied for a mortgage the fourth time, she had three months of work history in the United States. Most lenders reject that outright, without a second look at anything else in the file.

Here is what most buyers never find out. Beyond conventional financing there are alternative programs, and they exist for situations exactly like hers.

In Carin's case we connected her with a builder-backed loan program that fit her circumstances.

She closed on a brand new home. We also negotiated $6,000 toward her closing costs, a new refrigerator, a new washer and dryer, and window coverings included.

She is now preparing to rent out her basement to offset the mortgage payment, which is exactly the plan she walked in with.

What I want you to take from this: three lenders turning Carin down told her something about those three lenders. It did not tell her what was possible.

What these three have in common.

Nothing about any of them was the easy version of the deal. That is the only reason I got the call.

Somebody else had already said no

Four lenders, three lenders, a listing agent who would not counter. In every one of these, a professional had already closed the door before I got involved.

The answer was information, not pressure

A loan officer who reads a file differently. A listing history nobody else pulled. An alternative program most buyers never hear about. None of it was aggression.

It took longer than anyone wanted

February to May. Months of waiting through a foreclosure. A fourth mortgage application. Patience is most of the job, and it is the part nobody advertises.

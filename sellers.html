Most agents guess what buyers want. I am standing next to them.

I spend my week touring homes with buyers, around a hundred and seventy five of them in a year. I hear what people say in the basement when they think nobody is listening. I know which repairs end a conversation, which rooms get a second look, and what a buyer's agent is doing with a calculator in the driveway afterward.

Everything below comes from that side of the table. It is the part of selling that most listing presentations skip, because most listing agents are not in those houses.

The first ninety seconds decide more than you think

By the time a buyer reaches your kitchen, they have usually already formed an opinion. It happens at the curb, in the entry, and in the first room with real light.

What moves that opinion is rarely what sellers spend money on. It is smell before anything else, then whether the entry feels cramped, then how much natural light reaches the main living space, then clutter, which makes every room read as smaller than it is.

I have watched buyers walk back out of a house inside two minutes over a pet odour the seller had long since stopped noticing. That is a free fix that no amount of marketing spend can replace.

How a buyer and their agent actually value your house

They are not looking at your list price. They are pulling closed comparable sales and adjusting, and the adjustments are where your number gets made or lost.

Finished square footage is the big one. If your listing quotes total square footage including an unfinished basement, a sharp buyer's agent backs that space out and recalculates. Your house then looks expensive per foot against a comp that was quoted honestly. You can lose real money to a presentation choice rather than to the house itself.

They are also looking at concessions in the comps that never show in the headline price, how long each comp sat, and condition relative to yours. Understanding the math they are about to run is how you set a number that survives contact with it.

Why the first ten days decide the whole outcome

Your listing gets its largest audience in its first week. Everyone with a saved search matching your criteria sees it at once, including buyers who have been waiting months and are ready to move immediately.

Price it correctly and you are negotiating with the most motivated pool you will ever have. Price it high intending to come down later and you spend that window on buyers who decide you are overpriced and filter you out. By the time you reduce, the ready buyers have moved on, and the ones left are watching your days on market and waiting for the next cut.

Days on market is public. Every price reduction is public. Buyers read both as leverage, and they are right to.

Which improvements are worth doing before you list

The honest answer is that return on improvements varies by submarket, and anyone who gives you a national percentage is selling you something. What a finished basement returns in one Utah city is not what it returns twenty minutes up the freeway.

What travels is the ranking. Cleaning, decluttering and deodorising return more per dollar than any renovation, every time. Paint is next. After that, the things buyers visibly flinch at during a showing, which is usually flooring, dated light fixtures and anything that looks like deferred maintenance on the roof or the exterior.

What rarely returns what it costs right before a sale is a full kitchen or bathroom remodel. Buyers who want new finishes usually want their own choices, and the ones who do not want to pay for yours.

For your specific house, in your specific area, the answer needs comps rather than a rule of thumb. That is a conversation, not a blog post.

Reading an offer when it is not the highest number

The highest offer is not always the best offer, and sometimes it is not even the second best. What you are actually choosing is the offer most likely to close, on time, at close to the number on the page.

So you look at financing type and what it implies about the appraisal, how much earnest money is at risk and when it goes non-refundable, the length of the due diligence period, the financing and appraisal deadlines, and how much of your contingency protection the buyer has actually accepted.

A cash offer twenty thousand lower, with a short due diligence period and a closing date that matches your move, can easily be worth more than the top number from a buyer whose financing is thin and whose deadlines stretch into next quarter.

What happens after the inspection, and how to hold your position

Expect the buyer to come back with a request. Almost all of them do. The question is which parts of it are reasonable.

Items that are genuinely safety or structural, or that a lender will require, are worth addressing. A long list of cosmetic and maintenance items presented as a single number is a negotiation tactic, and it can usually be met with a counter rather than a cheque.

Your leverage in that moment depends on things decided weeks earlier: how your home was priced, how much interest it drew, and whether there is a credible second buyer. This is the clearest example of why pricing and preparation are not separate from negotiation. They are the negotiation, decided in advance.
